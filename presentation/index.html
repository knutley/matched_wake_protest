Matched wake analysis · worked example

A matched pair in the UK

Does police attention in an area change after a right-coded protest, compared with a left-coded protest held under the same conditions in the same police jurisdiction? Matched wake analysis answers this by pairing protests and counting what happens around each one. This page goes through one pair.

The two protests and every event around them are illustrative. They are generated to show how the method works, not taken from ACLED. The pooled estimates in sections 4 and 5 are the paper's.

1. Two protests, matched

Protest A is right-coded and protest B is left-coded. They are in the same police force area, months apart. Coarsened exact matching puts them in the same stratum: same jurisdiction and area type, same year, both on a Saturday, a similar distance from the nearest government building, a similar number of other protests in the jurisdiction that day, and similar protest histories beforehand.

2. Their wakes

Around each protest, draw a circle and look the same number of whole weeks back and forward. Protests on the same day are counted in neither period. The dots are other protests inside that space and time. A filled dot is one where police were present, which is the outcome. Hollow rings are police-attended protests before the matched protest, and the small grey dots are protests with no police reported.

Event density and police presence are exaggerated so the wakes are visible. In the real data, police are reported at about 4% of protests, and a 4 km wake rarely holds more than one police-attended protest a week. That is why the method pools thousands of pairs.

3. Counting before and after

For each protest, compare police-attended protests after it with those before it. The question is whether the change after A differs from the change after B. Using the before-period count controls for areas that simply have more policed protest to begin with.

One pair is noisy. Across all matched pairs, the paper estimates

Yposti = α + δ·Yprei + τ·Ti + εi

where Ti = 1 for a right-coded protest, weighted by the matching weights. τ is the difference-in-differences: how much the after-count around right-coded protests differs from that around matched left-coded protests, given the same before-count. It is reported as a percentage of the weighted average after-count around the matched left-coded protests. Because neighbouring wakes overlap, each model is re-estimated as a Poisson regression with standard errors clustered by NUTS 2 region.

4. From one pair to all of them

Pooled across France, Germany, Italy, Spain and the UK (2020–2024) and compared within police jurisdictions, right-coded protests are followed by fewer police-attended protests than matched left-coded protests in all 16 window–radius combinations. The gap is largest in the first week, at 12–17%. It narrows in weeks two and three and widens again at four weeks for the smaller radii. Most individual estimates are not statistically significant, and none is at the 5% level once errors are clustered. The evidence lies in the consistency of the direction.

5. Who protests next

Most of that gap comes from which protests follow. After a right-coded protest, more right-coded and fewer left-coded protests occur nearby, and right-coded protest is policed less wherever it happens, so police attention to the area dips. In the first week, right-coded follow-ups are also policed less often after a right-coded protest than after a left-coded one. Left-coded follow-ups are policed at the same rate whatever came before.
